10.1 What Is an API?

An API (Application Programming Interface) is a contract between a client and a server that specifies what data you can request, what parameters you can pass, and in what format you will receive the response. Unlike web scraping, where you reverse-engineer the HTML structure of a page designed for human readers, APIs provide structured, documented, and intentional access to data. They return clean JSON rather than cluttered HTML. They offer stable endpoints rather than fragile CSS selectors. And they come with documentation that tells you exactly what is available and how to ask for it.

The tradeoff is that APIs only expose what their operators choose to expose. You cannot query a field that the API does not provide. You cannot exceed the rate limits the server enforces. And as Chapter 3 describes, APIs can be restricted, repriced, or shut down entirely at the operator’s discretion. But when they are available, APIs are almost always preferable to scraping.

This chapter marks the transition from document scraping (Part II) to API-based data access (Part III). The skills you learn here — reading documentation, constructing parameterized URLs, handling pagination, parsing nested JSON responses — transfer directly to every API you will work with in subsequent chapters.

10.2 A Tour of Wikipedia

You have met Wikipedia’s article on the University of Colorado Boulder twice already. In Chapter 1, requests.get() fetched its HTML, and the pageviews API counted its readers. In Chapter 5, DevTools showed the requests a browser makes to draw it, and the Action API’s prop=info described it. This chapter returns to the same article, and to the city around it, Boulder, Colorado, to read Wikipedia the way its editors and researchers do.

Most readers see an article and nothing else. Behind every article sits a set of other pages and records that Wikipedia keeps in the open: who changed what and when, what editors argued about, how many people read it each day, what it links to and what links to it, and the same topic in other languages. Research on Wikipedia, including how it covers breaking news (Keegan et al. 2013; Keegan 2020) and how its editors make and change its rules (Keegan and Fiesler 2017), is built on these records. Each one has an API endpoint, so anything you can see in the interface, you can count. Wikipedia has kept these records open while commercial platforms closed theirs, which is why this book teaches APIs with it, as “Social History and Public Interest”, at the end of this chapter, describes.

10.2.1 The Article

The article itself is one page among several. The tabs above it lead to the others (Figure 10.1): the Article ①, its Talk page ②, and View history ③. The languages button ④ lists the editions that have an article on the same topic. Inside the text, each blue wikilink ⑤ points to another article. The box at the right is an infobox ⑥, filled in by a template: a reusable piece of wikitext that thousands of university articles share, so their infoboxes have the same fields in the same order.

10.2.2 Links In, Links Out, and Languages

A wikilink is a citation of another article, and the set of an article’s links is a small map of what its editors think it connects to. The reverse map is What links here (Figure 10.2): every page whose text links to this one ①, with filters for redirects, links, and transclusions ②. Interlanguage links (Figure 10.3) connect the same topic across Wikipedia’s editions: the menu’s search box ① and its list of editions ②. Each edition is written by its own community, so the articles are not translations of each other.

10.2.3 References

An article’s claims rest on its references (Figure 10.4): the References heading ① and the numbered citations under it ②. Each citation names a source, its publisher, and the date an editor retrieved it, and many add a link to an archived copy, the kind of capture Chapter 7 taught you to read. Wikipedia’s policy is that a claim someone might challenge needs a source a reader can check, so the references are the article’s evidence, and the links in them lead out of Wikipedia to the rest of the web.

In the API, the links in an article’s references, and anywhere else in its text, are prop=extlinks: one entry per external URL.

10.2.4 Categories and Templates

At the foot of the article, categories (Figure 10.5) ① file it with other pages: other universities founded in 1876, other flagship universities, other tourist attractions in Boulder. Each category is a page that lists its members, and categories sit inside larger categories, so together they make a rough map of what Wikipedia’s editors think belongs together. Templates are the reusable boxes you saw in the article: the infobox, the navigation boxes at the foot of the page, and the banners on the talk page below. An article that uses a template has the template’s text copied into it each time the page is drawn, which is why What links here can filter out “transclusions”. Both are kept by editors, so both are as consistent as the editors who maintain them.

In the API, an article’s categories are prop=categories and its templates are prop=templates. Some categories are hidden from readers, as “Getting Links, Categories, Templates, and Languages” shows.

10.2.5 The Talk Page

Every article has a talk page (Figure 10.6) where editors discuss changes before or after they make them. Its header ① says what the page is for and links the rules for discussing, such as assuming good faith, and the banners under it ② record the article’s quality rating and the WikiProjects that look after it. Below them are the discussions themselves, signed and dated, and older ones are moved to archives. Where an article’s revision history records what changed, its talk page often records why, and what editors disagreed about on the way.

A talk page is a page like any other, in the Talk namespace, so every request in this chapter works on it: ask for titles=Talk:University of Colorado Boulder.

10.2.6 The Revision History

The revision history (Figure 10.7) lists every edit ever made to the article, one row each: when ①, by whom ②, how many bytes it added or removed ③, and the editor’s summary ④. Editors’ usernames are public; many are pseudonyms, and edits without an account are listed under a temporary account name. Any past version can be opened, and any two compared, so the history is a complete record of how the article came to say what it says.

In the API, the history is prop=revisions, which “Getting Revisions”, below, pages through.

10.2.7 Pageviews

Wikipedia counts its readers, and publishes the counts. The Pageviews Analysis tool, linked from every article’s Page information, charts them (Figure 10.8): daily views ① and the period’s totals ②. The same numbers come from the REST pageviews API that “Getting Pageviews”, below, calls: the tool’s total for the quarter is the sum of the monthly counts for July, August, and September 2026 that the code in “What a Pageview Counts” prints.

10.2.8 User Pages and Contributions

Editors have pages too. A user page (Figure 10.9) is where an editor describes themself: here, Jimbo Wales, Wikipedia’s co-founder ① ②. Their contributions (Figure 10.10) list every edit they have made, across all of Wikipedia: when ①, to which page ②, by how many bytes ③, and with what summary ④.

10.2.9 From the Interface to the API

Each feature on the tour has an endpoint. Most are in the MediaWiki Action API, at https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php, and pageviews are in the Wikimedia REST API. The rest of the chapter calls them.

10.3 The Anatomy of an API Call

Every request to the MediaWiki Action API follows the same structure: a base URL, an action parameter specifying what you want to do, additional parameters narrowing your request, and a format parameter specifying the response format. Here is the request from Chapter 5 again:

import requests

url = "https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php"

params = {
    "action": "query",                          # we want to query for information
    "titles": "University of Colorado Boulder", # about this article
    "prop": "info",                             # specifically, basic info about the page
    "format": "json",                           # return the response as JSON
}

# Define these once near the top of your script and reuse them everywhere
CLIENT = "WebDataScience/1.0 (your-email@colorado.edu)"
HEADERS = {"User-Agent": f"{CLIENT} python-requests/{requests.__version__}"}

response = requests.get(url, params=params, headers=HEADERS)
data = response.json()

print(data)
# {'batchcomplete': '', 'query': {'pages': {'289672': {'pageid': 289672, 'ns': 0, 'title': 'University of Colorado Boulder', 'contentmodel': 'wikitext', 'pagelanguage': 'en', 'pagelanguagehtmlcode': 'en', 'pagelanguagedir': 'ltr', 'touched': '2026-10-15T21:52:24Z', 'lastrevid': 1380246879, 'length': 95273}}}}

The HEADERS constant deserves a comment before we go further. The Wikimedia User-Agent policy asks every client to identify itself with a descriptive User-Agent string that includes contact information. Since 2026, Wikimedia’s rate limits also depend on it: a client that identifies itself gets far more requests a minute than one that doesn’t. Put your own email address in place of your-email@colorado.edu, keeping the policy’s form: a name and version, your contact information in parentheses, then the library that sends the request and its version, here python-requests/ and the number requests.__version__ gives. CLIENT holds the first two parts on their own, because mwclient, later in this chapter, adds its own name and version to whatever you give it.

The same request works in a browser. Paste the address that requests builds into the address bar, with format=jsonfm in place of format=json, and the API lays its JSON out as a web page (Figure 10.11). Under pages is one key, the page’s ID ①, and under it the facts prop=info asks for, such as the article’s length in bytes ②. The API sandbox goes further: it builds a request from a form, with every module and parameter documented beside it, and shows the request it made, so you can copy it into Python.

The response is a nested JSON structure. You navigate it one level at a time:

# The data lives inside data > query > pages > {page_id}
pages = data["query"]["pages"]

# Page IDs are keys; take the first (and only) one
page_id = list(pages.keys())[0]
page_info = pages[page_id]

print(f"Title: {page_info['title']}")
print(f"Page ID: {page_info['pageid']}")
print(f"Length: {page_info['length']:,} bytes")
# Title: University of Colorado Boulder
# Page ID: 289672
# Length: 95,273 bytes

This nested navigation pattern — data["query"]["pages"][page_id] — is consistent across the MediaWiki API. Once you internalize it, every endpoint feels familiar.

10.4 Getting Revisions

Every edit ever made to a Wikipedia article is recorded and accessible through the API. The revisions endpoint returns metadata about each edit: who made it, when, how much the article size changed, and what edit summary they left. One request returns at most 500 revisions, so the function asks again until the API says there are no more:

import pandas as pd

def get_revisions(title, until="2026-09-30T23:59:59Z"):
    url = "https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php"
    params = {
        "action": "query",
        "titles": title,
        "prop": "revisions",
        "rvprop": "ids|timestamp|user|size|comment",
        "rvlimit": "max",   # 500 per request, the most the API allows
        "rvstart": until,   # newest edit to include, so the result doesn't change tomorrow
        "format": "json",
    }
    all_revisions = []
    while True:
        response = requests.get(url, params=params, headers=HEADERS)
        response.raise_for_status()               # stop on HTTP errors
        data = response.json()
        pages = data["query"]["pages"]
        page_id = list(pages.keys())[0]
        all_revisions.extend(pages[page_id].get("revisions", []))
        if "continue" in data:
            params.update(data["continue"])       # ask for the next batch
        else:
            break
    return pd.DataFrame(all_revisions)

cu_revisions = get_revisions("University of Colorado Boulder")
print(f"Total revisions: {len(cu_revisions):,}")
# Newest first, so the last three rows are the article's first edits
print(cu_revisions[["timestamp", "user", "size"]].tail(3).to_string(index=False))
# Total revisions: 2,988
#            timestamp        user  size
# 2003-12-23T07:08:15Z Dale Arnett  1232
# 2003-08-20T20:03:52Z        Dino  1171
# 2003-08-05T16:42:10Z        Dino  1037

The rvstart parameter fixes the newest edit the function will return, so running it next week gives the same answer as today. Fixing a time window is a habit worth keeping for any source that keeps changing.

10.4.1 Understanding Pagination

The MediaWiki API limits the number of results returned per request. When more results are available, the response includes a "continue" key with parameters you must add to your next request. This loop — request, extract, check for continuation, update parameters, repeat — is the standard pagination pattern for most APIs. You will see it again in Chapter 11 and Chapter 12. The EIA’s API in Chapter 11 pages the other common way: it reports how many rows match, and you ask for the next page by the number of rows to skip, offset, keeping count yourself instead of sending back a token.

The critical mistake newcomers make is forgetting to check for continuation. Without the while True loop and "continue" check, you only get the first batch of results — potentially a small fraction of the total dataset.

Two smaller details in get_revisions() are worth adopting as habits. The response.raise_for_status() call makes HTTP failures loud: if the server returns an error status, your code stops with an informative exception instead of failing confusingly when you try to parse the JSON. And for bulk jobs, API etiquette suggests adding "maxlag": 5 to your parameters, which tells overloaded Wikimedia servers to refuse your request with a retryable error so that automated traffic yields to human readers.

10.5 Getting Pageviews

The Wikimedia REST API, separate from the MediaWiki Action API, counts how many times an article was viewed. Its address is built from parts, like a file path: the project, the access method (desktop, mobile web, mobile app, or all), the agent (who is reading), the article, the granularity, and the dates. The function builds the address and returns a DataFrame:

def get_pageviews(title, start, end, agent="user", granularity="daily"):
    article = title.replace(" ", "_")
    url = (
        "https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/metrics/pageviews/per-article"
        f"/en.wikipedia/all-access/{agent}/{article}/{granularity}/{start}/{end}"
    )
    response = requests.get(url, headers=HEADERS)
    response.raise_for_status()  # stop on HTTP errors
    views = pd.DataFrame(response.json()["items"])
    views["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(views["timestamp"], format="%Y%m%d%H")
    return views[["timestamp", "views"]]

With the function defined, retrieving and plotting a series takes a few lines:

cu_views = get_pageviews("University of Colorado Boulder", "20210101", "20260930")
print(f"{len(cu_views):,} days, {cu_views['views'].sum():,} views")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.plot(cu_views["timestamp"], cu_views["views"], linewidth=0.8)
ax.set_title("Daily pageviews: University of Colorado Boulder")
ax.set_ylabel("Views by human readers")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
# 2,099 days, 1,367,292 views

Most days the article has about 600 human readers (Figure 10.13); the median from 2021 to September 2026 is 590. The spikes are events, and the tallest are football. Deion Sanders was hired as head coach on December 3, 2022, and the article had 5,042 views on December 5. The opening weeks of his first season, in September 2023, brought its busiest days: 16,767 views on September 17, after a double-overtime win over Colorado State that ended late on the night of the 16th in Colorado. That last date is a reminder that the API’s days are UTC days, so an evening in Colorado counts toward the next day.

10.5.1 What a Pageview Counts

A pageview is not a person. Wikimedia’s definition counts a request for a page’s content that succeeded, and leaves out edits, previews, and most special pages; one reader who reloads an article three times is three pageviews. The definition has also changed over time, and a long series crosses those changes:

  • The REST API’s counts begin in July 2015, under the current definition, drafted in 2014–15.
  • Before that, the hourly pagecounts-raw files, from December 2007 to 2016, counted every request for a page, reads and edits alike, including those from crawlers. They are a fallback for older history, and they are not comparable with the API’s counts.
  • The agent splits who is reading. user is meant to be people; spider is self-identified crawlers, such as search engines; and automated is traffic that Wikimedia’s analysts judged to be bots that don’t say so. The rules for that judgment change, as “Social History and Public Interest” describes, and when they do, past counts can be reclassified.

You can ask the API for each agent separately:

agents = []
for agent in ["user", "spider", "automated"]:

10.6 Getting Links, Categories, Templates, and Languages

The tour’s other features all come from the same endpoint, action=query, with a different prop or list, and they all paginate the same way. Rather than write one function per feature, write one function that runs any query and follows its continuation, and collects the pages it returns:

def query_all(params):
    url = "https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php"
    params = {"action": "query", "format": "json", **params}
    results = []
    while True:
        response = requests.get(url, params=params, headers=HEADERS)
        response.raise_for_status()  # stop on HTTP errors
        data = response.json()
        results.append(data["query"])
        if "continue" in data:
            params.update(data["continue"])
        else:
            break
    return results

def page_list(title, prop, limit_param):
    # One prop (links, categories, templates, extlinks, langlinks) for one page, as a list
    items = []
    for batch in query_all({"titles": title, "prop": prop, limit_param: "max"}):
        for page in batch["pages"].values():
            items.extend(page.get(prop, []))
    return items

Each feature on the tour is then one call:

title = "University of Colorado Boulder"

links = page_list(title, "links", "pllimit")
categories = page_list(title, "categories", "cllimit")
templates = page_list(title, "templates", "tllimit")
extlinks = page_list(title, "extlinks", "ellimit")
langlinks = page_list(title, "langlinks", "lllimit")

print(f"Wikilinks out: {len(links)}")
print(f"Categories: {len(categories)}")
print(f"Templates used: {len(templates)}")
print(f"External links: {len(extlinks)}")
print(f"Language editions: {len(langlinks)}")
print("First categories:", [c["title"] for c in categories[:3]])
# Wikilinks out: 650
# Categories: 40
# Templates used: 230
# External links: 217
# Language editions: 38
# First categories: ['Category:1876 establishments in Colorado Territory', 'Category:All Wikipedia articles written in American English', 'Category:All articles containing potentially dated statements']

The API counts 40 categories, more than the box at the foot of the article shows (Figure 10.5). The first ones it returns are why: “All Wikipedia articles written in American English” and “All articles containing potentially dated statements” are hidden categories, which editors use to track maintenance work and which readers don’t see. To leave them out, add "clshow": "!hidden" to the query.

What links here is a list, not a prop: it asks about all of Wikipedia rather than one page. Its results come under backlinks:

backlinks = []
for batch in query_all({"list": "backlinks", "bltitle": title, "bllimit": "max",
                        "blnamespace": 0}):    # namespace 0: articles only
    backlinks.extend(batch["backlinks"])
print(f"Articles that link to {title}: {len(backlinks)}")
# Articles that link to University of Colorado Boulder: 3294

The number of language editions an article appears in is a measure of global interest as perceived by Wikipedia’s volunteer editors. Articles about Western political leaders, major cities, and scientific concepts tend to appear in 100 or more language editions. Articles about topics primarily relevant to the Global South — local politicians, regional cultural practices, indigenous languages — may exist in only a handful. This asymmetry reflects the demographic composition of Wikipedia’s editor community, which skews heavily toward the Global North and toward speakers of European languages. When you use Wikipedia data for cross-cultural analysis, interlanguage link counts are a useful but imperfect proxy for a topic’s global visibility.

10.8 From Raw Requests to a Client: mwclient

This chapter has written its requests by hand, so that you can see every parameter and every continuation. In practice, most people who use the MediaWiki API from Python use a client library that does those parts for them. mwclient (documentation) is one of the simplest. It isn’t in webdata, so install it once, from the same conda-forge channel as the rest of the environment:

%conda install -y -c conda-forge mwclient

Then check that Python can import it, and which version you have:

import mwclient
print(mwclient.__version__)
# 0.10.1

conda-forge has version 0.10.1. The Python Package Index has a newer one, 0.11, which renames one argument used below: limit, the number of results to ask for in each request, becomes api_chunk_size. With 0.11, write api_chunk_size=500 where the code says limit=500.

site = mwclient.Site("en.wikipedia.org",
                     clients_useragent=CLIENT)
page = site.pages["University of Colorado Boulder"]

print(page.name, page.pageid, page.length)
print("Categories:", len(list(page.categories())))
print("Language editions:", len(list(page.langlinks())))

# Revisions, newest first, from the same moment get_revisions() started,
# 500 to a request (in mwclient 0.11, api_chunk_size=500)
revisions = list(page.revisions(start="2026-09-30T23:59:59Z", limit=500,
                                prop="ids|timestamp|user|size"))
print("Revisions:", len(revisions))
# University of Colorado Boulder 289672 95273
# Categories: 40
# Language editions: 38
# Revisions: 2988

Each line replaces one of this chapter’s functions. page.categories() and page.langlinks() are page_list(); page.revisions() is get_revisions(), continuation included; and site.usercontributions("Jimbo Wales") is the usercontribs query. mwclient also waits and retries when the servers ask it to (maxlag), and it can sign in, for scripts that edit. Its user guide has more examples, and Pywikibot is the larger library that Wikipedia’s bot operators use.

One thing mwclient doesn’t do, in version 0.10.1 or 0.11, is wait out a 429. When Wikimedia’s rate limit answers with status 429, mwclient raises an HTTPError, and the loop over page.revisions() stops partway. Wait for the number of seconds in the response’s retry-after header, then run it again; “Common Issues to Debug”, below, has more on the limits.

Two things stay raw. mwclient talks to the Action API only, so pageviews still come from get_pageviews(). And a client is a convenience, not a substitute for the documentation: when you need a parameter the library doesn’t expose, you are back to the Action API’s documentation and a request you write yourself.

10.9.1 Combining Endpoints

The real analytical power of the API emerges when you combine endpoints. A natural question is whether editing and reading move together: do articles get edited more when more people are reading them?

boulder_revisions["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(boulder_revisions["timestamp"])

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(8, 5), sharex=True)
for name, revs, views in [(title, cu_revisions, cu_views),
                          (boulder, boulder_revisions, boulder_views)]:
    # Revision timestamps are in UTC; drop the time zone to match the pageviews.
    # Revisions arrive newest first, so count by month before keeping 2021 on
    edits = (revs.set_index(revs["timestamp"].dt.tz_localize(None))
                 .resample("ME").size().loc["2021":])
    monthly_views = views.set_index("timestamp")["views"].resample("ME").sum()
    axes[0].plot(monthly_views.index, monthly_views.values, label=name)
    axes[1].plot(edits.index, edits.values, label=name)
    print(f"{name}: monthly views and edits correlate at {monthly_views.corr(edits):.2f}")
axes[0].set_ylabel("Monthly views")
axes[1].set_ylabel("Monthly edits")
axes[0].legend()
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

The city’s busiest month, March 2021, had 120,790 views, five times its usual month: on March 22, a gunman killed ten people at a King Soopers grocery store in Boulder, and the article had 63,327 views the next day. Reading and editing move together only loosely: over the 69 months, monthly views and edits correlate at 0.21 for the university and 0.38 for the city. When news breaks, a burst of readers brings editors with it (Keegan et al. 2013). But many edits keep their own schedule, such as cleanups, reorganizations, and arguments no reader set off. The city’s most edited month, September 2021, with 45 edits, had an ordinary number of readers.

10.12 Social History and Public Interest

Wikipedia is the counter-example to the enclosure story of Chapter 3. While commercial platforms restricted, repriced, or shut down their APIs, Wikipedia kept three things open: its API; its content, licensed for free reuse under CC BY-SA 4.0; and complete database dumps of every public wiki for anyone to download. That combination is why this book uses Wikipedia to teach API fundamentals. It follows from the mission of the Wikimedia Foundation, which hosts the site: to “collect and develop educational content under a free license or in the public domain, and to disseminate it effectively and globally” (Wikimedia Foundation). Since 2001 Wikipedia has grown into the world’s largest reference website, written by volunteers in more than 300 languages; in October 2026 the English edition alone counted about 269,000 contributors who had edited in the past month (Wikipedia:About, List of Wikipedias). Its open records have made it one of the most studied sites on the web: of how strangers coordinate when news breaks (Keegan et al. 2013; Keegan 2020), of how a community writes and rewrites its own rules (Keegan and Fiesler 2017), and of how facts written there travel to search engines and other platforms (Ford 2022).

But openness does not mean representativeness. Research has documented a demographic skew in who edits Wikipedia: editors are disproportionately male, from the Global North, and technically fluent. In the best-known survey of editors, from 2008, about one in six was a woman, even after correcting for who chose to answer (Hill and Shaw 2013). This translates into content gaps: articles about women, people of color, and the Global South are systematically shorter, less detailed, and less well-referenced than articles about men and Western topics (Mesgari et al. 2015), and biographies of women are written and linked differently from biographies of men (Wagner et al. 2016). WikiProject Women in Red has worked on the gender gap since July 2015, when it was founded after editors found that only 15.5% of English Wikipedia’s biographies were about women. It organizes edit-a-thons and keeps “redlists” of notable women who have no article; by its count, the share of biographies about women passed 20% in December 2024. Data retrieved from the API reflects these structural patterns: any Wikipedia-based analysis inherits the biases of its volunteer editor community.

Since 2024, a newer pressure has come from the same openness that makes Wikipedia useful. Its free content is training data and source material for generative AI, and three changes followed.

Crawlers. In April 2025, the Foundation reported that the bandwidth used to download images and other media had grown 50% since January 2024, and that the growth came from crawlers rather than human readers. Bots made up about 35% of pageviews but at least 65% of the traffic that was most expensive to serve. The Foundation’s summary: “our content is free, our infrastructure is not” (Mueller, Danis, and Lavagetto, 2025). A year later it reported that it was blocking or throttling about 30% of automated requests from crawlers that ignore its policies, and that it had begun rolling out the API rate limits in “Common Issues to Debug”, below, which reward a client that identifies itself (2026).

Readers. In 2025 the Foundation found that much of an unusual rise in apparent human traffic was bots built to evade detection. It updated its bot detection, reclassified its traffic data for March to August 2025, and reported that human pageviews over those months were roughly 8% lower than in the same months of 2024. It attributed the decline to “the impact of generative AI and social media on how people seek information”: answers that draw on Wikipedia without a visit (Miller, 2025). That reclassification is why “What a Pageview Counts”, above, warns that past counts can change. Fewer visits also means fewer readers who become editors or donors.

Writing. Editors have had to decide what to do with text that language models write. WikiProject AI Cleanup, formed in December 2023, works “to identify text written by AI, and scrutinize such text to make sure it follows Wikipedia’s policies” (WikiProject AI Cleanup). A speedy-deletion criterion, G15, lets administrators delete pages that could only plausibly have been generated by a language model without review. In March 2026, after a community request for comment, a new guideline, Writing articles with large language models, prohibited using them to generate or rewrite article content, with exceptions for basic copyediting of one’s own writing and for translation. The Foundation’s own AI strategy, published in April 2025, put its investment in tools for the volunteers, such as help for moderators and translation, rather than in machine-written articles (Albon and Zia, 2025).

For a data scientist, these changes are both a subject and a responsibility. The pageview series you retrieve now carries a definition that changed in 2025. The articles you read may have been written, in part, under new rules about machines. And your own code is part of the automated traffic the Foundation is managing: identify yourself, make requests one at a time, fix your time windows so you don’t fetch the same data twice, and use the database dumps for anything large.

10.13 Common Issues to Debug

  • Pagination: The single most common mistake. Always check for "continue" in the response. Without it, you get only the first batch (often 500 or fewer results out of thousands).
  • Nested JSON navigation: The MediaWiki API nests data several levels deep. Print data.keys(), then data["query"].keys(), then data["query"]["pages"].keys() to navigate step by step.
  • Titles: The Action API accepts titles with spaces; the REST pageviews API wants underscores, which is why get_pageviews() swaps them. For titles with other special characters (slashes, question marks), encode them with urllib.parse.quote(title, safe="").
  • Rate limiting (status 429): Wikimedia counts requests per minute across the Action API and the REST APIs, pageviews included: 200 a minute for a client whose User-Agent follows the policy, and 10 a minute for one that doesn’t, shared with everyone else on its network address (Wikimedia APIs/Rate limits; the limits are new in 2026 and may change). Past the limit, the answer is status 429, with a retry-after header that says how many seconds to wait; wait that long before trying again. The practical rule: serial requests, follow continue tokens, maxlag for bulk work, and always an informative User-Agent.
  • Missing pages: If you request an article that does not exist, the page ID will be negative (e.g., -1). Always check for this before trying to access revision data.

10.14 Key Takeaways

APIs provide structured, documented, intentional access to web data. Every feature Wikipedia shows a reader — talk pages, revision histories, pageviews, links, categories, templates, languages, user contributions — has an endpoint, so what you can see you can count. Learning one API well gives you a transferable skill: read the documentation, construct parameterized requests, handle pagination with continuation tokens, and parse nested JSON one level at a time. A client library such as mwclient does the repetitive parts once you know what they are. Know what your numbers count: a pageview’s definition has changed, and so has the traffic behind it. Wikipedia remains a model of open, mission-driven data access, and one now under pressure from the automated traffic that open access invites.

## What Is an API?

An API (Application Programming Interface) is a contract between a client and a server that specifies what data you can request, what parameters you can pass, and in what format you will receive the response. Unlike web scraping, where you reverse-engineer the HTML structure of a page designed for human readers, APIs provide structured, documented, and intentional access to data. They return clean JSON rather than cluttered HTML. They offer stable endpoints rather than fragile CSS selectors. And they come with documentation that tells you exactly what is available and how to ask for it.

The tradeoff is that APIs only expose what their operators choose to expose. You cannot query a field that the API does not provide. You cannot exceed the rate limits the server enforces. And as @sec-post-api describes, APIs can be restricted, repriced, or shut down entirely at the operator's discretion. But when they are available, APIs are almost always preferable to scraping.

This chapter marks the transition from document scraping (Part II) to API-based data access (Part III). The skills you learn here — reading documentation, constructing parameterized URLs, handling pagination, parsing nested JSON responses — transfer directly to every API you will work with in subsequent chapters.

### Categories and Templates

At the foot of the article, **categories** (@fig-article-categories) ① file it with other pages: other universities founded in 1876, other flagship universities, other tourist attractions in Boulder. Each category is a page that lists its members, and categories sit inside larger categories, so together they make a rough map of what Wikipedia's editors think belongs together. **Templates** are the reusable boxes you saw in the article: the infobox, the navigation boxes at the foot of the page, and the banners on the talk page below. ### The Talk Page

Every article has a **talk page** (@fig-talk-page) where editors discuss changes before or after they make them. Its header ① says what the page is for and links the rules for discussing, such as assuming good faith, and the banners under it ② record the article's quality rating and the WikiProjects that look after it. Below them are the discussions themselves, signed and dated, and older ones are moved to archives. ## The Anatomy of an API Call

Every request to the MediaWiki [Action API](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/API:Action_API) follows the same structure: a base URL, an `action` parameter specifying what you want to do, additional parameters narrowing your request, and a `format` parameter specifying the response format. Here is the request from @sec-protocols again:

```{python}
import requests

url = "https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php"

params = {
    "action": "query",                          # we want to query for information
    "titles": "University of Colorado Boulder", # about this article
    "prop": "info",                             # specifically, basic info about the page
    "format": "json",                           # return the response as JSON
}

# Define these once near the top of your script and reuse them everywhere
CLIENT = "WebDataScience/1.0 (your-email@colorado.edu)"
HEADERS = {"User-Agent": f"{CLIENT} python-requests/{requests.__version__}"}

response = requests.get(url, params=params, headers=HEADERS)
data = response.json()

print(data)
# {'batchcomplete': '', 'query': {'pages': {'289672': {'pageid': 289672, 'ns': 0, 'title': 'University of Colorado Boulder', 'contentmodel': 'wikitext', 'pagelanguage': 'en', 'pagelanguagehtmlcode': 'en', 'pagelanguagedir': 'ltr', 'touched': '2026-10-15T21:52:24Z', 'lastrevid': 1380246879, 'length': 95273}}}}
```

The `HEADERS` constant deserves a comment before we go further. The [Wikimedia User-Agent policy](https://foundation.wikimedia.org/wiki/Policy:Wikimedia_Foundation_User-Agent_Policy) asks every client to identify itself with a descriptive User-Agent string that includes contact information. Since 2026, Wikimedia's [rate limits](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Wikimedia_APIs/Rate_limits) also depend on it: a client that identifies itself gets far more requests a minute than one that doesn't. Put your own email address in place of `your-email@colorado.edu`, keeping the policy's form: a name and version, your contact information in parentheses, then the library that sends the request and its version, here `python-requests/` and the number `requests.__version__` gives. `CLIENT` holds the first two parts on their own, because mwclient, later in this chapter, adds its own name and version to whatever you give it.

The same request works in a browser. Paste the address that `requests` builds into the address bar, with `format=jsonfm` in place of `format=json`, and the API lays its JSON out as a web page (@fig-api-response). Under `pages` is one key, the page's ID ①, and under it the facts `prop=info` asks for, such as the article's length in bytes ②. The [API sandbox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:ApiSandbox) goes further: it builds a request from a form, with every module and parameter documented beside it, and shows the request it made, so you can copy it into Python.

![The chapter's first request in a browser, with `format=jsonfm`, October 2026. The page's ID ① is the key under `pages`, and `length` ② is the article's size in bytes.](images/ch-10/api-response_annotated.png){#fig-api-response .lightbox fig-alt="Screenshot of the MediaWiki API's HTML view of a JSON response. The response is a nested JSON structure. You navigate it one level at a time:

```{python}
# The data lives inside data > query > pages > {page_id}
pages = data["query"]["pages"]

# Page IDs are keys; take the first (and only) one
page_id = list(pages.keys())[0]
page_info = pages[page_id]

print(f"Title: {page_info['title']}")
print(f"Page ID: {page_info['pageid']}")
print(f"Length: {page_info['length']:,} bytes")
# Title: University of Colorado Boulder
# Page ID: 289672
# Length: 95,273 bytes
```

This nested navigation pattern — `data["query"]["pages"][page_id]` — is consistent across the MediaWiki API. Once you internalize it, every endpoint feels familiar.

## Getting Revisions

Every edit ever made to a Wikipedia article is recorded and accessible through the API. The revisions endpoint returns metadata about each edit: who made it, when, how much the article size changed, and what edit summary they left. One request returns at most 500 revisions, so the function asks again until the API says there are no more:

```{python}
import pandas as pd

def get_revisions(title, until="2026-09-30T23:59:59Z"):
    url = "https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php"
    params = {
        "action": "query",
        "titles": title,
        "prop": "revisions",
        "rvprop": "ids|timestamp|user|size|comment",
        "rvlimit": "max",   # 500 per request, the most the API allows
        "rvstart": until,   # newest edit to include, so the result doesn't change tomorrow
        "format": "json",
    }
    all_revisions = []
    while True:
        response = requests.get(url, params=params, headers=HEADERS)
        response.raise_for_status()               # stop on HTTP errors
        data = response.json()
        pages = data["query"]["pages"]
        page_id = list(pages.keys())[0]
        all_revisions.extend(pages[page_id].get("revisions", []))
        if "continue" in data:
            params.update(data["continue"])       # ask for the next batch
        else:
            break
    return pd.DataFrame(all_revisions)

cu_revisions = get_revisions("University of Colorado Boulder")
print(f"Total revisions: {len(cu_revisions):,}")
# Newest first, so the last three rows are the article's first edits
print(cu_revisions[["timestamp", "user", "size"]].tail(3).to_string(index=False))
# Total revisions: 2,988
#            timestamp        user  size
# 2003-12-23T07:08:15Z Dale Arnett  1232
# 2003-08-20T20:03:52Z        Dino  1171
# 2003-08-05T16:42:10Z        Dino  1037
```

The `rvstart` parameter fixes the newest edit the function will return, so running it next week gives the same answer as today. Fixing a time window is a habit worth keeping for any source that keeps changing.

### Understanding Pagination

The MediaWiki API limits the number of results returned per request. When more results are available, the response includes a `"continue"` key with parameters you must add to your next request. This loop — request, extract, check for continuation, update parameters, repeat — is the standard pagination pattern for most APIs. You will see it again in @sec-government and @sec-social. The EIA's API in @sec-government pages the other common way: it reports how many rows match, and you ask for the next page by the number of rows to skip, `offset`, keeping count yourself instead of sending back a token.

The critical mistake newcomers make is forgetting to check for continuation. Without the `while True` loop and `"continue"` check, you only get the first batch of results — potentially a small fraction of the total dataset.

Two smaller details in `get_revisions()` are worth adopting as habits. The `response.raise_for_status()` call makes HTTP failures loud: if the server returns an error status, your code stops with an informative exception instead of failing confusingly when you try to parse the JSON. And for bulk jobs, API etiquette suggests adding `"maxlag": 5` to your parameters, which tells overloaded Wikimedia servers to refuse your request with a retryable error so that automated traffic yields to human readers.

## Getting Pageviews

The Wikimedia REST API, separate from the MediaWiki Action API, counts how many times an article was viewed. Its address is built from parts, like a file path: the project, the access method (desktop, mobile web, mobile app, or all), the **agent** (who is reading), the article, the granularity, and the dates. The function builds the address and returns a DataFrame:

```{python}
def get_pageviews(title, start, end, agent="user", granularity="daily"):
    article = title.replace(" ", "_")
    url = (
        "https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/metrics/pageviews/per-article"
        f"/en.wikipedia/all-access/{agent}/{article}/{granularity}/{start}/{end}"
    )
    response = requests.get(url, headers=HEADERS)
    response.raise_for_status()  # stop on HTTP errors
    views = pd.DataFrame(response.json()["items"])
    views["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(views["timestamp"], format="%Y%m%d%H")
    return views[["timestamp", "views"]]
```

With the function defined, retrieving and plotting a series takes a few lines:

### What a Pageview Counts

A pageview is not a person. Wikimedia's [definition](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:Page_view) counts a request for a page's content that succeeded, and leaves out edits, previews, and most special pages; one reader who reloads an article three times is three pageviews. The definition has also changed over time, and a long series crosses those changes:

- **The REST API's counts begin in July 2015**, under the current definition, drafted in 2014–15.
- **Before that**, the hourly [pagecounts-raw](https://dumps.wikimedia.org/other/pagecounts-raw/) files, from December 2007 to 2016, counted every request for a page, reads and edits alike, including those from crawlers. They are a fallback for older history, and they are not comparable with the API's counts.
- **The agent splits who is reading.** `user` is meant to be people; `spider` is self-identified crawlers, such as search engines; and `automated` is traffic that Wikimedia's analysts judged to be bots that don't say so. The rules for that judgment change, as "Social History and Public Interest" describes, and when they do, past counts can be reclassified.

You can ask the API for each agent separately:

## Getting Links, Categories, Templates, and Languages

The tour's other features all come from the same endpoint, `action=query`, with a different `prop` or `list`, and they all paginate the same way. Rather than write one function per feature, write one function that runs any query and follows its continuation, and collects the pages it returns:

```{python}
def query_all(params):
    url = "https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php"
    params = {"action": "query", "format": "json", **params}
    results = []
    while True:
        response = requests.get(url, params=params, headers=HEADERS)
        response.raise_for_status()  # stop on HTTP errors
        data = response.json()
        results.append(data["query"])
        if "continue" in data:
            params.update(data["continue"])
        else:
            break
    return results

def page_list(title, prop, limit_param):
    # One prop (links, categories, templates, extlinks, langlinks) for one page, as a list
    items = []
    for batch in query_all({"titles": title, "prop": prop, limit_param: "max"}):
        for page in batch["pages"].values():
            items.extend(page.get(prop, []))
    return items
```

Each feature on the tour is then one call:

```{python}

categories = page_list(title, "categories", "cllimit")
templates = page_list(title, "templates", "tllimit")
extlinks = page_list(title, "extlinks", "ellimit")
langlinks = page_list(title, "langlinks", "lllimit")

print(f"Wikilinks out: {len(links)}")
print(f"Categories: {len(categories)}")
print(f"Templates used: {len(templates)}")
print(f"External links: {len(extlinks)}")
print(f"Language editions: {len(langlinks)}")
print("First categories:", [c["title"] for c in categories[:3]])
# Wikilinks out: 650
# Categories: 40
# Templates used: 230
# External links: 217
# Language editions: 38
# First categories: ['Category:1876 establishments in Colorado Territory', 'Category:All Wikipedia articles written in American English', 'Category:All articles containing potentially dated statements']
```

The API counts 40 categories, more than the box at the foot of the article shows (@fig-article-categories). The first ones it returns are why: "All Wikipedia articles written in American English" and "All articles containing potentially dated statements" are **hidden categories**, which editors use to track maintenance work and which readers don't see. To leave them out, add `"clshow": "!hidden"` to the query.

What links here is a `list`, not a `prop`: it asks about all of Wikipedia rather than one page. Its results come under `backlinks`:


The [Python Package Index](https://pypi.org/project/mwclient/) has a newer one, 0.11, which renames one argument used below: `limit`, the number of results to ask for in each request, becomes `api_chunk_size`. With 0.11, write `api_chunk_size=500` where the code says `limit=500`.

```{python}
site = mwclient.Site("en.wikipedia.org",
                     clients_useragent=CLIENT)
page = site.pages["University of Colorado Boulder"]

print(page.name, page.pageid, page.length)
print("Categories:", len(list(page.categories())))
print("Language editions:", len(list(page.langlinks())))

# Revisions, newest first, from the same moment get_revisions() started,
# 500 to a request (in mwclient 0.11, api_chunk_size=500)
revisions = list(page.revisions(start="2026-09-30T23:59:59Z", limit=500,
                                prop="ids|timestamp|user|size"))
print("Revisions:", len(revisions))
# University of Colorado Boulder 289672 95273
# Categories: 40
# Language editions: 38
# Revisions: 2988
```

Each line replaces one of this chapter's functions. `page.categories()` and `page.langlinks()` are `page_list()`; `page.revisions()` is `get_revisions()`, continuation included; and `site.usercontributions("Jimbo Wales")` is the `usercontribs` query. mwclient also waits and retries when the servers ask it to (`maxlag`), and it can sign in, for scripts that edit. Its [user guide](https://mwclient.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user/index.html) has more examples, and [Pywikibot](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Manual:Pywikibot) is the larger library that Wikipedia's bot operators use.

One thing mwclient doesn't do, in version 0.10.1 or 0.11, is wait out a 429. When Wikimedia's rate limit answers with status 429, mwclient raises an `HTTPError`, and the loop over `page.revisions()` stops partway. Wait for the number of seconds in the response's `retry-after` header, then run it again; "Common Issues to Debug", below, has more on the limits.

Two things stay raw. mwclient talks to the Action API only, so pageviews still come from `get_pageviews()`. And a client is a convenience, not a substitute for the documentation: when you need a parameter the library doesn't expose, you are back to the [Action API's documentation](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/API:Action_API) and a request you write yourself.

### Combining Endpoints

The real analytical power of the API emerges when you combine endpoints. A natural question is whether editing and reading move together: do articles get edited more when more people are reading them?

```{python}
boulder_revisions["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(boulder_revisions["timestamp"])

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(8, 5), sharex=True)
for name, revs, views in [(title, cu_revisions, cu_views),
                          (boulder, boulder_revisions, boulder_views)]:
    # Revision timestamps are in UTC; drop the time zone to match the pageviews.
    # Revisions arrive newest first, so count by month before keeping 2021 on
    edits = (revs.set_index(revs["timestamp"].dt.tz_localize(None))
                 .resample("ME").size().loc["2021":])
    monthly_views = views.set_index("timestamp")["views"].resample("ME").sum()
    axes[0].plot(monthly_views.index, monthly_views.values, label=name)
    axes[1].plot(edits.index, edits.values, label=name)
    print(f"{name}: monthly views and edits correlate at {monthly_views.corr(edits):.2f}")
axes[0].set_ylabel("Monthly views")
axes[1].set_ylabel("Monthly edits")
axes[0].legend()
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
# University of Colorado Boulder: monthly views and edits correlate at 0.21
# Boulder, Colorado: monthly views and edits correlate at 0.38
```

## Social History and Public Interest

Wikipedia is the counter-example to the enclosure story of @sec-post-api. While commercial platforms restricted, repriced, or shut down their APIs, Wikipedia kept three things open: its [API](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/API:Main_page); its content, licensed for free reuse under [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Copyrights); and complete [database dumps](https://dumps.wikimedia.org/) of every public wiki for anyone to download. That combination is why this book uses Wikipedia to teach API fundamentals. It follows from the mission of the Wikimedia Foundation, which hosts the site: to "collect and develop educational content under a free license or in the public domain, and to disseminate it effectively and globally" ([Wikimedia Foundation](https://wikimediafoundation.org/who-we-are/mission/)). Since 2001 Wikipedia has grown into the world's largest reference website, written by volunteers in more than 300 languages; in October 2026 the English edition alone counted about 269,000 contributors who had edited in the past month ([Wikipedia:About](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:About), [List of Wikipedias](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/List_of_Wikipedias)). Its open records have made it one of the most studied sites on the web: of how strangers coordinate when news breaks [@keegan2013hot; @keegan2020encyclopedia], of how a community writes and rewrites its own rules [@keegan2017evolution], and of how facts written there travel to search engines and other platforms [@ford2022writing].

But openness does not mean representativeness. Research has documented a demographic skew in who edits Wikipedia: editors are disproportionately male, from the Global North, and technically fluent. In the best-known survey of editors, from 2008, about one in six was a woman, even after correcting for who chose to answer [@hill2013wikipedia]. This translates into content gaps: articles about women, people of color, and the Global South are systematically shorter, less detailed, and less well-referenced than articles about men and Western topics [@mesgari2015sum], and biographies of women are written and linked differently from biographies of men [@wagner2016women]. [WikiProject Women in Red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:WikiProject_Women_in_Red) has worked on the gender gap since July 2015, when it was founded after editors found that only 15.5% of English Wikipedia's biographies were about women. It organizes edit-a-thons and keeps "redlists" of notable women who have no article; by its count, the share of biographies about women passed 20% in December 2024. Data retrieved from the API reflects these structural patterns: any Wikipedia-based analysis inherits the biases of its volunteer editor community.

Since 2024, a newer pressure has come from the same openness that makes Wikipedia useful. Its free content is training data and source material for generative AI, and three changes followed.

**Crawlers.** In April 2025, the Foundation reported that the bandwidth used to download images and other media had grown 50% since January 2024, and that the growth came from crawlers rather than human readers. Bots made up about 35% of pageviews but at least 65% of the traffic that was most expensive to serve. The Foundation's summary: "our content is free, our infrastructure is not" ([Mueller, Danis, and Lavagetto, 2025](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2025/04/01/how-crawlers-impact-the-operations-of-the-wikimedia-projects/)). A year later it reported that it was blocking or throttling about 30% of automated requests from crawlers that ignore its policies, and that it had begun rolling out the API rate limits in "Common Issues to Debug", below, which reward a client that identifies itself ([2026](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2026/03/26/quo-vadis-crawlers-progress-and-whats-next-on-safeguarding-our-infrastructure/)).

**Readers.** In 2025 the Foundation found that much of an unusual rise in apparent human traffic was bots built to evade detection. It updated its bot detection, reclassified its traffic data for March to August 2025, and reported that human pageviews over those months were roughly 8% lower than in the same months of 2024. It attributed the decline to "the impact of generative AI and social media on how people seek information": answers that draw on Wikipedia without a visit ([Miller, 2025](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2025/10/17/new-user-trends-on-wikipedia/)). That reclassification is why "What a Pageview Counts", above, warns that past counts can change. Fewer visits also means fewer readers who become editors or donors.

**Writing.** Editors have had to decide what to do with text that language models write. WikiProject AI Cleanup, formed in December 2023, works "to identify text written by AI, and scrutinize such text to make sure it follows Wikipedia's policies" ([WikiProject AI Cleanup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:WikiProject_AI_Cleanup)). A speedy-deletion criterion, G15, lets administrators delete pages that could only plausibly have been generated by a language model without review. In March 2026, after a community request for comment, a new guideline, [Writing articles with large language models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Writing_articles_with_large_language_models), prohibited using them to generate or rewrite article content, with exceptions for basic copyediting of one's own writing and for translation. The Foundation's own AI strategy, published in April 2025, put its investment in tools for the volunteers, such as help for moderators and translation, rather than in machine-written articles ([Albon and Zia, 2025](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2025/04/30/our-new-ai-strategy-puts-wikipedias-humans-first/)).

For a data scientist, these changes are both a subject and a responsibility. The pageview series you retrieve now carries a definition that changed in 2025. The articles you read may have been written, in part, under new rules about machines. And your own code is part of the automated traffic the Foundation is managing: identify yourself, make requests one at a time, fix your time windows so you don't fetch the same data twice, and use the database dumps for anything large.

## Common Issues to Debug

- **Pagination**: The single most common mistake. Always check for `"continue"` in the response. Without it, you get only the first batch (often 500 or fewer results out of thousands).
- **Nested JSON navigation**: The MediaWiki API nests data several levels deep. Print `data.keys()`, then `data["query"].keys()`, then `data["query"]["pages"].keys()` to navigate step by step.
- **Titles**: The Action API accepts titles with spaces; the REST pageviews API wants underscores, which is why `get_pageviews()` swaps them. For titles with other special characters (slashes, question marks), encode them with `urllib.parse.quote(title, safe="")`.
- **Rate limiting (status 429)**: Wikimedia counts requests per minute across the Action API and the REST APIs, pageviews included: 200 a minute for a client whose User-Agent follows the policy, and 10 a minute for one that doesn't, shared with everyone else on its network address ([Wikimedia APIs/Rate limits](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Wikimedia_APIs/Rate_limits); the limits are new in 2026 and may change). Past the limit, the answer is status 429, with a `retry-after` header that says how many seconds to wait; wait that long before trying again. The practical rule: serial requests, follow `continue` tokens, `maxlag` for bulk work, and always an informative User-Agent.
- **Missing pages**: If you request an article that does not exist, the page ID will be negative (e.g., `-1`). Always check for this before trying to access revision data.

## Key Takeaways

APIs provide structured, documented, intentional access to web data. Every feature Wikipedia shows a reader — talk pages, revision histories, pageviews, links, categories, templates, languages, user contributions — has an endpoint, so what you can see you can count. Learning one API well gives you a transferable skill: read the documentation, construct parameterized requests, handle pagination with continuation tokens, and parse nested JSON one level at a time. A client library such as mwclient does the repetitive parts once you know what they are. Know what your numbers count: a pageview's definition has changed, and so has the traffic behind it. Wikipedia remains a model of open, mission-driven data access, and one now under pressure from the automated traffic that open access invites.
